Presentations
  1. "Ruling for the Majority, Cementing Hegemony: A Comparative Study of High Courts and National Language Policy in France, the United States, and China." Pi Sigma Alpha National Student Research Conference, American University, Washington, DC, February 2026.
    Abstract

    Language policies constitute a fundamental dimension of state consolidation and national identity formation, yet judicial institutions remain underexamined as potential agents of linguistic hegemony. This comparative study investigates how high courts in France, the United States, and China, three states representing distinct trajectories within past, present, and emerging global hegemony, moderate majority-language dominance through judicial action. Through analysis of distinct judicial modalities—entrenchment, restraint, and foreclosure—this research demonstrates that courts consistently privilege majority languages while constraining or foreclosing minority-language rights protections. The paper situates these divergent national strategies within broader frameworks of hegemonic anxiety and linguistic capital, revealing how judicial choices translate anxiety about linguistic status into jurisprudential positions that reinforce linguistic hierarchy. Through comparative analysis, this research suggests that courts serve as critical mediators between state sovereignty, majority-language dominance, and minority-language rights, thereby potentially (re)shaping the relationship between linguistic capital and geopolitical power amid transitions in global hegemony.

  2. "Comparative Legal Linguistics of French and American Judicial Opinions: A Corpus-Based Analysis of Linguistic Style in the Civil and Common Law." Undergraduate Research and Creative Activities (URECA) Day, Wake Forest University, September 2025. Richter Scholars Program; supervised by Dr. Margaret Bender.
    Abstract

    This study examines comparative legal linguistics of French and American judicial opinions, employing corpus-based approaches to analyze linguistic features in apex court decisions from the French Cour de cassation and the U.S. Supreme Court. Leveraging two large corpora comprising 123 million tokens from French arrêts (1963–2023) and 22 million tokens from U.S. majority opinions (1960–2019), it investigates features such as word count, lexical diversity, and passive voice usage across the common and civil law legal traditions. My Richter Scholarship-funded research, conducted in Parisian law libraries and supported by established theoretical and qualitative frameworks, and enhanced by corpus analysis, indicates that judicial discourse may reflect or shape legal culture in subtle ways. Findings suggest potential tendencies and linguistic differences: U.S. Supreme Court opinions tend to be longer on average and may evolve differently over time compared to French judgments, which could be attributed to differences in judicial reasoning and institutional practices. The implementation of corpus linguistics tools, such as AntConc, is presented as a means of moving beyond traditional qualitative analysis, with the potential to systematically uncover institutional linguistic practices. It is proposed that judicial language could encode elements of social and institutional dynamics, although the scope and implications of this relationship remain open to further investigation. The relative scarcity of comprehensive, comparative, quantitative studies of French and American courts presents an opportunity for corpus-driven approaches to contribute to deeper insights into the interplay of language, law, and tradition at the highest levels of judicial decision-making across legal cultures.

  3. "Linguistic Reflections of Institutional Tradition and Function: An Exploratory Corpus-Driven Analysis of the Cour de cassation and Conseil d'État." Undergraduate Conference on Languages and Cultures, Elon University, September 2025.
    Abstract

    Legal language serves as a specialized form of communication that shapes legal interpretation, with judicial decisions representing particularly formalized textual genres within the broader realm of legal discourse. While comparative legal studies have extensively explored institutional differences within and between legal systems, the linguistic manifestation of these functions and traditions at a large-scale quantitative level remains underexplored. This research address that gap by examining the linguistic differences between French Cour de cassation and Conseil d'État decisions through corpus-based analysis, investigating how language structures reflect their distinct jurisdictional roles and historical evolution within the civil law tradition. While both constitute apex courts within the French organisation juridictionnelle, their functional differences create unique linguistic patterns that manifest institutional identity and judicial reasoning approaches. Using mixed-methods corpus linguistics, this study analyzes structural, stylistic, and functional linguistic features in corpora comprising officially published court decisions (arrêts) as well as preparatory documents that constitute the unofficial discourse of the courts. Key linguistic variables include passive voice frequency, citation density, modality, and syntactic and lexical complexity. The findings will highlight how linguistic structure corresponds to each court’s approach to legal reasoning and style and their relationship to precedent, authority, and institutional design. A comparative component contextualizes these findings in relation to US Supreme Court discourse, highlighting how intra-traditional functional differences compare to inter-traditional variations. This research contributes to legal linguistics by demonstrating how language both constructs and reflects institutional identity within legal systems, advancing intercultural understanding of both French judicial culture and comparative legal discourse.
